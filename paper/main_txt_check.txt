Summary
Beijing's dockless bike-share system in May 2017 was not short of bicycles. It was short of bicycles in the right place at the right time. Using 3.21 million orders from 484,754 bicycles and 349,241 users over fourteen days, this paper builds one behavioural model and uses it to answer four operational questions on a common footing. The central finding is a diagnosis of oversupply. Capture-recapture between the nine complete days and the truncated second window places the closed fleet at 495,540 bicycles (model interval 484,754-506,635), serving 4.33 rides each per day. At the initial-inventory proxy of 343,638 bicycles the 50-zone program leaves only 432.23 departures unserved, so the binding problem is cost and placement, not shortage; at cell resolution the simulated unserved share is 5.84% at 343,638 and 3.51% at 495,540. These statements are conditional on the modelled inventory. Behaviour (Q1). Trips form a marked spatio-temporal point process: a non-homogeneous Poisson stream, a singly-constrained gravity destination choice with time-varying decay, and a speed-based duration. Decay ranges from 2.01 (morning commutes) to 2.52 (midday errands). On two held-out days log-loss is 3.24- 3.52 against 3.28-3.64 for distance-only and 7.9-8.2 for attraction-only; top-five destinations are predicted 40-48% of the time. Same-cell trips are overestimated (12.83% modelled against 8.89% observed, +3.94 percentage points), not matched. Five latent user types describe the hourly profile. Rebalancing (Q2). An integer time-space network program moves bicycles between 50 zones, and a samezone tricycle layer, solved after it, moves them between cells. The single-layer truck baseline uses 60 trucks to move 24,949 bicycles per day over 5,659.17 truck-kilometres at 83,807.74 CNY/day. Adding 975 tricycles brings the two-tier cost to 617,055.7515 CNY/day, a saving of 902,223.20 (59.385%) against the no-tricycle cell-level baseline, leaving 39,984.2332 departures unserved. The integer plan violates no constraint; its LP gap is 1.01%. Incentives (Q3). The answer has two sides. Against the truck layer the instrument fails: at  = 2 the best mixed policy is at least 1,532 CNY/day worse than pure trucks even at zero reward, because the truck layer's own residual is only 432.23 unserved departures. Against the tricycle layer it succeeds: at  = 0.60 it saves 18,011.46 CNY/day (2.92%), pays 16,608.52 inside the 30,000 budget, cuts the fleet from 975 to 959 and unmet from 39,984.2332 to 35,019.77. The two source caps bracket the saving at 1.08%-2.92%. E-fences (Q4). Parking demand is the arrival process on top of the standing inventory. A greedy maximalcoverage layout on the capacity-coverage frontier has its Kneedle knee at 4,000 sites of 150 slots (600,000 slots), covering 59.53% of peak occupancy and 55.80% of arrivals at 343,638, and 52.18% and 49.59% at 495,540; 394,553.13 bike-slots are unassigned at the measured inventory. The 705-fence solution is retained only as an uncapped capacity reference. Overlap is small (multiplicity 1.0213, duplicate capacity 2.80%), and a hold-out check gives cell correlation 0.804, L1 0.423 and top-100 overlap 32. Competition (Q5). At cell resolution, splitting the market equally raises unmet from 11.66% to 12.10% even with no spatial mismatch, and mismatch raises it to 18.99%; the 12.10% is a share of within-fence departures, not of the whole city. Independent fences need 1.08-1.13 times the capacity of shared fences, but sharing fences does not reduce lost orders, because the fleets remain separate. The durable damage of competition is duplicated capital, trucks, land and, most damagingly, the data needed to run any model above.

Keywords: dockless bike sharing; gravity model; time-space network; linear programming; discrete choice; maximal coverage; Beijing.

1 Introduction
1.1 A city that rides, and the problem that follows
Between 2016 and 2018, dockless shared bicycles changed how Chinese cities moved. Unlike the earlier station-based systems of Hangzhou and Shanghai, a dockless fleet imposes no docking constraint: a rider may leave the bicycle wherever the journey ends, and the next rider finds it through a phone application. The convenience is real, and so is the externality. Because no station absorbs the end of a trip, the spatial distribution of bicycles is simply the integral of every rider's private choice, and that distribution drifts. Bicycles pile up at subway exits, at shopping centres and at the foot of office towers, while residential streets empty by nine in the morning. Operators respond with trucks; cities respond with parking rules; and both are expensive because neither is guided by a model of where the bicycles will go. This paper builds that model from the largest artefact the industry left behind: the 2017 Mobike Algorithm Challenge dataset, which records 3,213,310 orders placed on 484,754 bicycles by 349,241 users in Beijing between 10 and 24 May 2017. Each order carries an order identifier, a user identifier, a bicycle identifier, a start time, a bicycle type, and origin and destination coordinates encoded as seven-character geohashes. There is no inventory record, no station, and no record of a user who wanted to ride and found no bicycle.
1.2 The five questions
The problem poses five questions, which we restate in operational terms:
Q1. Behaviour. Build a mathematical model of how users ride: when they start, where they go, and how long it takes.
Q2. Rebalancing. Design an optimal repositioning and scheduling plan for a typical day, using trucks or tricycles to move bicycles between areas.
Q3. Incentives. Explore a scheme that pays users to end their trips where the platform wants bicycles. Q4. E-fences. Predict parking demand by location and propose a layout and capacity plan for electronic
fences. Q5. Competition. Discuss how the coexistence of competing platforms affects the resolution of the prob-
lems above.
1.3 Three things the data do not tell us
Most of the analytical difficulty in this problem is not in the optimisation; it is in three quantities the dataset does not contain and that a careless model would silently assume. No inventory. The data record movements, not stocks. A cell that receives no arrivals may be empty or may hold fifty bicycles that nobody rode. Any statement about a "shortage" therefore requires an assumption about where the bicycles were at midnight, and that assumption is not identified by the data (Section 9). Unknown sampling rate. The file is described as a sample of the operator's orders. If only a fraction  of orders is present, then every count in the file is a count of 1/ of reality, and the fleet is larger than the number of distinct bicycle identifiers. We estimate  from the internal consistency of successive rides of the same bicycle, and we show that the fleet estimate is invariant to it. Truncated demand. A user who wants to ride and finds no bicycle never appears. The observed trips are therefore demand censored at supply, which biases any naive estimate of demand downward exactly where the system is most stressed. Our models avoid this bias by treating observed trips as realisations of a rate process rather than as the demand itself, and by reporting service quality as a simulated quantity rather than a fitted one.
1.4 Related work
Bike-share research divides into three strands. The first documents the systems and their users [1, 2]; studies of station-based and free-floating systems add land-use effects on bicycle flows [12], predictors of sharedbicycle use [13], and dockless travel patterns in Singapore and Nanjing [10, 11]. The second treats rebalancing as a vehicle-routing or inventory problem: static repositioning [3], the static rebalancing problem with multiple vehicles [4], inventory-plus-routing formulations with service-level constraints [5], and stationless static rebalancing for free-floating systems [9]. The third replaces trucks with prices, either by dynamic redistribution with user incentives [6] or by mechanism design for balanced systems [7]. The probabilistic analysis of station occupancy that underlies both is given in [8]. Our approach differs from all three in one respect: the behavioural model is not a convenience for generating

1.5 Executive summary: contributions and answers
The contributions are four.

1. A diagnosis. The Beijing system of May 2017 was oversupplied. We establish this three ways -- a capture-recapture fleet estimate, a zone-level stockout diagnostic, and a cell-level simulation -- and it reframes Q2 from "prevent shortages" to "minimise the cost of restoring balance".
2. One model, four uses. A single estimated gravity model feeds the rebalancing LP, the incentive supply curve and the parking forecast, so the four answers are internally consistent.
3. An incentive supply curve. Rather than assert an acceptance rate, we derive the cost of voluntary relocation as a function of the reward, solve an hourly assignment program with one reroute per trip, and price the instrument against both layers: it cannot replace the trucks, and it can replace part of the tricycle layer.
4. Explicit uncertainty. The unidentified quantities are bounded, not assumed: the sampling rate is estimated and its role as an upper-bound scale factor is stated, the initial-inventory proxy is carried as two scenarios, and every behavioural parameter is swept in Section 9.

2.1 Assumptions
A1. Stationary weekday regime. The seven weekdays between 10 and 19 May that are fully covered by the sample are exchangeable draws from one daily regime. Weather, holidays and price promotions are not modelled. The observed daily volume varies with a coefficient of variation of 8.25% around its mean (Fig. 1a), so any single-day quantity in this paper carries that order of sampling noise.
A2. Poisson trip generation. Departures from cell  in time interval  are Poisson with rate , constant on 15-minute intervals. This is the standard assumption for individual trip-making; pooled across cells the counts are over-dispersed relative to Poisson, which reflects heterogeneity in the rates rather than a failure of the conditional model.
A3. Supply-independent destination choice. A rider chooses a destination without regard to whether a bicycle will be available there. This is the usual assumption of trip-distribution modelling. It is conditional here: the 50-zone LP finds no zone stockout at the measured inventory, but the cell-level simulation finds 5.84% unserved at the initial-inventory proxy 343,638 and 3.51% at 495,540, so the censoring is small but not zero and is reported rather than assumed away.
A4. Endogenous attraction. The attraction of a destination is measured by the arrivals it actually receives. The model is therefore a description of an equilibrium, not a structural model of land use: it may be used to predict how flows respond to distance and to time of day, but not to predict the consequence of changing what is built at a destination. We flag this again in Section 10.
A5. Homogeneous, reliable bicycles. All bicycles in service are usable; a bicycle identifier persists until the bicycle is retired. The dataset reports a bicycle type, which is constant per bicycle and therefore carries no information for our purposes.
A6. Truck operations. A truck carries at most  = 40 bicycles, travels one inter-zone leg per hour, and unloads entirely at its destination. Loading and unloading are assumed to fit inside the same hour. Trucks may serve only the eight nearest zones within 9 km, a restriction that keeps the arc set to 290 and matches the range of an overnight shift.
A7. Utility of accepting an incentive. A rider offered  CNY to end at a different cell evaluates  =  -  () + , with () the extra travel and walking time for a detour , and  logistic. The parameters ,  and the inertia constant 0 are not identified from our data: the order file contains no reward records, although Mobike had been operating red-envelope (hongbao) bikes since March 2017 [30, 31]. They are fixed at  = 2,  = 0.35, 0 = 0.8 throughout; only 0 is re-optimised (at 0.8 and 1.2 times its value) in Section 9, and the absence of a full ,  sweep is carried as a limitation.
A8. Parking demand equals arrivals, on a standing stock. A parked bicycle occupies space where its trip ended. Parking demand at a location is therefore the arrival process, on top of whatever bicycles already stand there; capacity is the peak of that path, not the peak of arrivals from an empty start.
A9. Cost parameters. A bicycle costs 1.0 CNY per day to own and maintain (about 1,000 CNY over a threeyear life); a truck costs 600 CNY per day to own, insure and staff, and 6 CNY per kilometre to run. These are planning assumptions of the right order for 2017 Beijing. They are perturbed one at a time over ±50% in Section 9; the truck-kilometre price alone moves the Q2 total by 33,534.39 CNY/day over 3-9 CNY/km, and Section 9 ranks every input this way. All costs are linear in the other parameters.
A10. Two-tier repositioning. Trucks move bicycles between zones as in A6. Tricycles move bicycles between cells within one zone only, carrying tri = 20 bicycles at 15 km/h with 0.5 minutes per bicycle handled, trips of at most 5 km, operating 07:00-20:00, at 300 CNY per tricycle-day and 0.2 CNY per kilometre. Because reroutes do not cross a zone boundary, the truck layer is unchanged by the tricycle layer. The tricycle allocation is integer, and the executable plan raises the fractional LP fleet to the smallest fixed point at which every zone's schedule can be run.
A11. Two ledgers, one budget. A reward can be settled on the increment ledger, paying  per completed reroute that fills a deficit (bill  ), or on the payment ledger, paying  per accepted invitation including riders who would have gone there anyway (bill  ). The 30,000 CNY daily budget is a promise and the two ledgers price it differently; every result is labelled by its ledger. The payment ledger is enforced by reserving  per invitation and stopping when the cumulative reserve reaches the budget, which is conservative and may truncate the program; at the Q3 optimum the increment-ledger bill is checked against the budget rather than truncated by it.
A12. Placement and payment. A reroute is placed in the hour of the original trip and in the same zone; no

3 Data
3.1 The order file
The dataset is the order file released for the 2017 Mobike Algorithm Challenge [29]. The dataset contains 3,214,096 order records. Each record has an order identifier, a user identifier, a bicycle identifier, a bicycle type, a start timestamp and two seven-character geohashes that encode the start and end positions. Decoding follows the standard base-32 latitude/longitude interleaving [25]: each character contributes five bits, alternately to longitude and latitude, so that a seven-character string resolves a rectangle of roughly 153 × 153 m. The decoded coordinates agree with the operator's own city, and 99.6% of the records begin with the geohash prefix wx4, which covers Beijing. We remove 786 records whose decoded start lies outside the metropolitan bounding box (39.4-40.4N, 115.8- 117.0E); these are GPS or decoding faults as far away as 20N. After the filter 3,213,310 orders remain, placed by 349,241 users on 484,754 distinct bicycles.
3.2 Coverage, and why the analysis uses seven days
Fig. 1a shows the daily volume. Three features matter. The volume is stable at 225,000-322,000 orders per day from 10 to 19 May, with the expected weekend dip. 17 May is entirely absent -- not sparse, absent -- so no interpolation is possible and we treat the day as missing. From 20 May onward the sample is truncated, not merely quieter. The five days carry 54.53% of the typicalday volume and 63.11% of the typical-day bicycles, and 13 entire hour blocks are missing (20 May lacks 19:00-22:00; 23 May lacks three hours; 24 May lacks six). Within the hours that are present, the hourly profile correlates only 0.54-0.72 with a typical day, against 0.98-0.997 among the complete weekdays. These are the signatures of a sample cut off, not of behavioural change, so we do not use 20-24 May for estimation. All estimation uses the nine recorded days of 10-19 May, and within them the seven fully covered weekdays, which we call typical days. Weekends are used only where the text says so.

3.3 The sampling rate
The file is a sample of the operator's orders, not a census. If each order is retained independently with probability , then every count must be divided by , and the number of distinct bicycle identifiers understates the fleet. We estimate  from the bicycles themselves. For a given bicycle, consider two consecutive orders that both appear in the file. They are adjacent in the bicycle's true history exactly when no order between them was dropped, which for independent retention happens with probability . Adjacency is observable: if the two orders are adjacent, the bicycle was not moved in between, so the second order must start in the same 153 m geohash cell in which the first ended. Conversely, a mismatch proves that at least one order is missing. Across 2,003,318 consecutive pairs, 12.83% satisfy the exact end-equals-next-start test; 17.72% are within 160 m and 19.97% within 250 m. Placebo pairs that skip one or two observed trips, and are therefore known to be separated by a missed ride, match at only 2.30% and 1.30% within 250 m. Correcting the exact-match rate for that false match gives   0.18- 0.19; we use the bracket   [0.128, 0.200] and the central value 0.185 in the discussion below. We retain  = 0.128 for the absolute flow quantities. This is the lower end of the bracket, so dividing by 0.128 overstates every absolute flow by a factor of about 0.185/0.128 = 1.45 relative to the central estimate. All absolute counts in this paper are therefore upper bounds; ratios, shares and the fleet estimate (which is invariant to ) are unaffected. Finite-sample resampling is a different matter from the algebraic invariance: at a 5% rate, 64.5% of 200 draws deviate by more than 4% from the full-sample value, and the maximum deviation is 17.9%; only at the 50% rate does the maximum fall below 4% (3.67%). What holds at every rate is the invariance of the estimator, not small finite-sample scatter.

The estimator assumes the two windows sample the same closed population, and the closure failures are larger than the sampling error and point in a particular direction. Heterogeneity in use biases the pooled estimate down: the recapture rate rises monotonically from 0.4945 for one-trip bicycles to 0.8039 for 11-or-more-trip bicycles, so heavy users are over-represented in the overlap. Retirement of a recapturable bicycle biases it up by 0.10533 per bicycle; new deployment between the windows biases it up by 1.49339 per bicycle; retirement of a never-used bicycle has no effect. A stratified Chapman estimator returns the observed 14-day union exactly, so only   484,754 is design-independent. As a model interval we therefore report that union as the lower end, 484,754, and the normalised nine-day unseen-share method as an upper reference, 506,635. The 5/16 00:00 drop-off snapshot gives 343,638 bicycles. We call this the initial-inventory proxy: the stock implied by each bicycle's last observed return position, not a fleet count; it is used only as 0 in Q2, Q4 and Q5. At 495,540 bicycles and 2.14 × 106 rides per day, each bicycle is ridden 4.33 times per day; because the fleet estimate is biased low, 4.33 is an upper estimate, 4.42 is the design-independent ceiling at   484,754, and 4.01 holds only in the 534,039 scenario. Individual bicycles sit idle even when the fleet is abundant in the aggregate; that is the tension the rest of the paper quantifies.

3.5 Spatial units
Two partitions are used, at different resolutions and for different purposes. Cells. A grid of 0.005 (about 555 × 426 m) is laid over the city. Keeping only cells with at least 50 sampled trips leaves 5,263 cells, which contain 95.81% of the typical-day trips. Cells are the unit at which a rider can reasonably be asked to change destination (a few hundred metres), so they are used for the incentive model (Q3) and the parking model (Q4). Zones. Fifty zones are formed by -means on the cell centroids weighted by trip volume, and each cell is assigned to its nearest centroid. Zones hold between 604 and 12,119 trips per day and are roughly 4 km across, which is the scale at which a truck moves bicycles. They are used for rebalancing (Q2). 4.5 Duration
Ride duration is generated from speed rather than modelled directly. This is an explicit assumption, not an estimated relationship, and the order file contains no end time from which a duration could be observed. A bicycle is assumed to travel 1.35 times the straight-line distance; its speed is approximately normal with mean 11.5 km/h and standard deviation 2.5, clipped to [6, 20] km/h to remove the implausible tails; and one minute is added for locking and unlocking. 5.4 The tricycle layer: replacing within-zone walking with a fleet
The truck plan equalises zone totals. Inside a zone, riders can already borrow a bicycle up to 300 m away, but that walking option cannot move a bicycle across a cell boundary when the neighbouring cell is more than one cell-centre away; on this grid the 300 m radius reaches no neighbour centre. The tricycle layer is therefore a same-zone vehicle-routing problem: a tricycle carries tri = 20 bicycles, travels at 15 km/h, needs 0.5 minutes per bicycle handled, makes trips of at most 5 km, and operates from 07:00 to 20:00. Its cost is 300 CNY per tricycle-day, 0.2 CNY per vehicle-kilometre, and the same 6 CNY penalty on any cell-level departure left unserved. The layer is solved zone by zone as an integer routing program; the truck layer is held fixed at the  = 60 plan. Solved as an LP over tricycle counts, the layer buys 889 tricycles, costs 507,779.67 and leaves 40,041.58 cell-level departures unserved, for a total of 591,587.42 and a saving of 927,691.53 (61.06%) against the notricycle cell-level baseline of 1,519,278.95. That is an ideal LP upper bound, not a plan: the integer schedule built from the same allocation leaves 534 of 13,775 trips unassigned in 15 zones, because a fractional tricycle cannot be dispatched. Raising the fleet to the smallest executable fixed point adds 86 tricycles across those 15 zones, for 975 tricycles and zero unassigned trips in all 50 zones. The executable layer costs 533,248.01, of which 293,342.61 is fixed, kilometre and handling cost and 239,905.40 is the 6 CNY penalty on 39,984.2332 unserved departures. The total is 617,055.7515 CNY per day, a saving of 902,223.20 (59.385%) against the 1,519,278.95 no-tricycle baseline. Counting actual rather than full loads raises the layer to 534,278.98 and the total to 618,086.73, a saving of 901,192.22 (59.317%); the 1,030.98 difference (0.17%) is the load-factor approximation and is carried as a range, not hidden. The executable plan remains preferred until the unserved penalty falls to 1.47215 CNY per bicycle or the tricycle fixed cost rises to 1,225.36 CNY per day. The integer cost of the tricycle layer is visible in the rounding. At the city scale the LP leaves 38,489.44 cell departures unserved and the integer schedule 39,984.2332, a rounding loss of 1,494.80. In zone 24, the largest allocation (44 tricycles), the LP leaves 2,059.49 and the integer schedule 2,085.98; the 26.50 loss is 0.43% of the bicycle flows the LP assigns in the zone and is bounded by 81.5. The bound is not tight, but it is small relative to the layer cost, and the direction is known: the executable plan can only be worse than its LP relaxation.

5.6 Uncertainty and the limits of the number
The numbers above are a planning midpoint, not an expectation or a median. Propagating the grouped parameter draws of Section 9 through the LP proxy gives a Q2 total of 617,056 at the midpoint, with a joint P5-P95 interval of 271,732-882,188 and a median of 484,372; the planning midpoint sits above the distribution median because the cost parameters enter multiplicatively and the reference is a point, not the mean. Executable unmet is 39,984.23 at the midpoint, with a joint interval of 2,789-73,744 and a median of 11,392. The saving share is 0.594 at the midpoint, with a joint interval of -0.461-0.692 and a median of 0.260: in 31 of 128 joint draws (24%) the two-tier plan is worse than the no-tricycle baseline, because the cost parameters that make tricycles cheap also make the truck layer expensive. The truck-fleet optimum is 60 at the reference, but the joint P5-P50-P95 is 0-40-60, and the grid distribution is 0 in 8 draws, 20 in 37, 40 in 72, 60 in 9 and 80 in 2; only 11 of 128 draws (8.6%) choose 60 or more, so about 91% of the joint draws change the recommended truck fleet. Two boundaries follow. The 975-tricycle fleet is an upper bound on the executable minimum (the ideal LP uses 889), and the 59.385% saving is a lower bound on the ideal LP saving (61.06%); both are reported as bounds, not as point estimates. The intervals are LP-proxy intervals: at 11 representative parameter points the integer chain deviates from the proxy by at most 38,986.30 CNY per day, about 0.2 standard deviations of the Q2 total, so the interval is a shape statement, not a calibrated confidence interval.

6 Question 3: Paying Riders to Move Bicycles
6.1 Two instruments at two scales
A truck can carry a bicycle ten kilometres between zones. A rider can be persuaded to end a trip a few hundred metres from where she intended, and no more: the reward would have to be absurd to make someone ride across the city. The two instruments therefore operate at different scales, and the right question is not which one replaces the other but how much of each layer the incentive can absorb. The answer turns out to be asymmetric, and we report it as two paragraphs rather than one headline. The first paragraph prices the incentive against the truck layer: the layer the incentive cannot replace, because that layer's residual is already negligible. The second prices it against the tricycle layer, which exists precisely because the zone-level truck plan cannot see within-zone mismatch. The two calibers share the same acceptance function, the same hourly assignment program and the same budget; they differ in what is downstream of the reroute.

6.3 Incentives cannot replace trucks
The truck layer of Q2 is a single-layer plan. Its own residual is tiny: at  = 0 the zone model leaves 432.23 departures unserved, and at  = 2 it leaves 33.19. There is no large pool of truck-served unmet demand for an incentive to substitute for, so the only question is whether the incentive can pay for itself by reducing the truck objective. It cannot. Solving the integer truck program of Section 5 with and without the incentive at  = 0, 2, 5, 10, 20, re-solving both sides at a 0.1% gap, gives a payment ceiling pure - hybrid that is negative at every : -1,739.22 at  = 0, -1,591.40 at  = 2, -1,588.76 at  = 5, -1,623.33 at  = 10 and -1,528.82 at  = 20. A negative ceiling means no non-negative reward can break even. The zero-payment result is the cleanest statement. At  = 2, even with the reward set to zero, the best mixed policy costs at least 1,532 CNY per day more than pure trucks: the returned difference is 1,591.40 and the mixed solve carries its own 59.06 CNY gap, so 1,532 is the gap-robust lower bound, 11.1 times the 0.1% solver tolerance of 138.32. Equivalently, the 30,000 CNY budget is 150.6 times the total penalty value of all 33.19 remaining unmet departures (6 CNY per departure, 199.16 CNY in total); even taking the 1% upper bound of 134.81 bicycles rather than the 33.19, the penalty value is 809 CNY and the budget is 37.1 times it. And forcing zero unmet requires the policy of 312,289.21 CNY/day, 2.2578 times the pure-truck cost. The incentive removes an estimated 10.4 zone-level unmet trips at  = 2, but that difference is the difference of two returned solutions whose gaps are 66.13 and 59.06 CNY -- about 11 and 10 bicycles at 6 CNY -- so the sign is not resolvable within solver tolerance; we do not use it. The structural reason is one of scale: a reroute moves a trip between cells, while the truck objective prices zone-hour unmet. Only the part of the reroute that changes a zone-hour arrival total can remove a zone-level unmet trip, and at  = 2 that part is negligible. The incentive is therefore not a substitute for trucks at any fleet size tested. This conclusion is about the truck layer; it says nothing about the tricycle layer.

6.6 The scheme, and where its numbers stop
The scheme that follows is narrow and explicit.
1. A reward of 0.60 CNY per accepted reroute, posted on a 0.05-CNY grid, with the arrivals-cap plateau a single grid point and the surplus-cap plateau 0.55-1.15. The reward is uniform across users; a personalised reward is not modelled.
2. A target list, not a target point. The reward is offered for ending in a cell on the hourly deficit list, recomputed from the demand model of Section 4. The reroute is placed in the hour of the original trip.
3. Payment on completion, settled on the increment ledger, with the payment ledger and its reservation rule reported alongside so that the budget promise can be audited.

7.2 Where to put the fences: the Kneedle rule
Siting is maximal coverage. A fence at cell  with walking radius  = 450 m serves every arrival whose destination lies within  of ; the planner chooses a set of fences to cover as many arrivals as possible. The objective is monotone and submodular, so the greedy algorithm -- repeatedly place the fence that covers the most still-uncovered arrivals -- attains at least (1 - 1/) = 63.2% of the optimum [24]. With 5,263 cells as candidate sites the pass runs in seconds. The radius is a planning threshold, not a 2017 measured walking distance; the OSM check below is geographic, not behavioural. The number of fences is chosen as a knee of the capacity-coverage frontier. Let  be the number of open sites and () the occupancy coverage of the capped -site plan; both series are normalised to [0, 1] over the candidate range, and the selected plan is the point whose normalised (, ) pair lies farthest from the chord joining the first and last frontier points (the Kneedle rule). It is the point where additional fences stop buying coverage at the frontier's average rate. It is not the minimiser of a cost function: with every site capped at 150 slots, slots() = 150 and cost() = slots() +  is monotone increasing, so it has no interior minimum. The exchange rate  is used only for the uncapped reference below, where a fence has no hard size limit. On the capped frontier the knee is 4,000 sites, with a normalised deviation of 0.21829 from the chord; at the 495,540 inventory the knee is also 4,000 (deviation 0.20982). The marginal curve explains why: coverage rises by about 1.5 percentage points per 100 sites up to 3,500, by 1.19 points between 3,500 and 4,000, then by 0.26 points to 4,500 and 0.05 points to 5,000. Beyond 4,000, sites are expensive and buy little.

7.4 Replay: which service failure occurs
A static coverage share does not say what happens during a day. We replay the 4,000-site plan in 15-minute steps with the simulated arrivals and departures, counting two distinct failures: a full site turns away an arriving bicycle (overflow), and an empty site cannot lend one (departure shortfall). On the design day at 343,638 the plan realizes 55.80% arrival coverage, with zero full sites, zero overflow and a departure shortfall of 132,250.84: the failure is emptiness, not congestion. With one-hour steps the realized coverage falls by 0.08 percentage points to 55.73%, overflow appears at 1,617.94 bicycles, and the shortfall rises to 158,257.23. At 495,540 the design day realizes 49.59%, with zero overflow and a shortfall of 4,277.25; one-hour steps give 49.46%, overflow 2,709.12 and shortfall 38,829.33. The two held-out days are the stress test. At 343,638 the 12 May replay realizes 50.33% with overflow 104,551.04, shortfall 123,832.54 and 1,007 full sites; 19 May realizes 50.35% with overflow 126,920.81, shortfall 152,736.70 and 1,110 full sites. One-hour steps move coverage by -0.25 and -0.41 percentage points and raise the shortfall by 22,736.57 and 35,096.42. Full sites therefore appear only on the higherdemand test days, while the design day fails by emptiness; a coarser step creates some overflow on the design day but never enough to change that reading. The finer step is the more favourable of the two, so the capacity conclusion does not depend on it.

8 Question 5: What Competition Does to the Problem
8.1 Three things competition can break
The five questions above were answered for a single operator holding the whole market. Competition can affect availability, parking capacity and information, and the three are not the same kind of claim. Availability is a stochastic statement about stockouts; parking capacity is a deterministic statement about slots; information is a statement about what any single firm can estimate. We simulate the second at the resolution the Q4 plan actually has, and treat the first and third as arguments whose direction we can state and whose magnitude we cannot.
8.2 A cell-resolution mismatch experiment
The Q4 plan is fixed: 4,000 sites, 150 slots each,  = 450 m, initial-inventory proxy 343,638. Two platforms share the market equally (share  = 0.5), each drawing its own Poisson arrivals and departures at half rate, each holding half of every cell's standing inventory. Three institutions are compared: · independent -- each platform owns a 150-slot fence at each open site and only its own bicycles park there; · independent_equal -- the same, but 75 slots per platform, so the concrete per site equals the shared
regime's 150 and the comparison isolates pooling rather than total capacity; · shared -- one 150-slot fence per site, both platforms park in it, riders still take only their own bicycles,
and overflow is rationed in proportion to that step's arrivals. Sharing pools slots, not fleets. A spatial mismatch is then imposed at four intensities   {0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5}: platform A's share of each origin cell's trips is ( + 2), where  is the cell's east-west coordinate scaled to [-1, 1] and  is bisected so that A still carries half of all trips. At  = 0 the split is proportional and is the model-consistent control; at  = 1.5 A's riders are concentrated in the east while its fleet remains at half of every cell's inventory. Sharing fences does not reduce lost orders. The shared regime is not better than the independent one: at  = 0 it is 12.12% against 12.10%, and at   1.0 it is slightly worse than independent_equal (16.11% against 16.00%, 19.55% against 19.03%). Capacity duplication has a price. The full-site rates separate the institutions cleanly. The independent regime almost never fills a fence (0.00007% to 0.57% of site-steps), because each platform's own fence sits half-empty while the other platform's riders walk past it. The independent_equal regime, with the same total concrete split in two, fills up to 6.23% of site-steps, and the shared regime sits between them at 0.35% to 1.57%. A low full-site rate is therefore not evidence of adequate capacity; it is evidence that the capacity is in the wrong hands.
8.4 Cost and data duplication
Cost. The rebalancing plan of Q2 is computed over the whole city. A platform with a third of the trips still faces the same 290 arcs and the same nightly window, but fills each truck with a third as many bicycles. The fixed component of the truck fleet -- 600 CNY per truck per day, which is 42.96% of the daily cost at  = 60 (36,000 of 83,807.74 CNY) -- is therefore incurred by every platform separately. The same argument applies

23

to the tricycle layer's fixed cost, 300 CNY per tricycle-day, which is 293,342.61 of the 617,055.7515 two-tier total. Data. This is the most serious loss and the least visible. Every model in this paper is estimated from the full origin-destination matrix. A platform holding a third of the market observes a third of the rows, so the attraction  of Eq. (3) and the cell deficit  used in Eq. (19) are estimated with a standard error that grows as 3. More decisively, no platform can compute the plans of Q2 and Q4 at all, because the Q2 truck plan is defined on a city-wide zone network, the Q4 layout on a city-wide demand surface, and the Q3 reward on a city-wide deficit list. Each is a public-good computation that only the city can run.
8.5 Multi-homing, and why it does not rescue the situation
If riders use several applications, availability may improve: a rider who finds no bicycle on one platform may find one on another, so effective density is closer to the sum of the platforms' densities. But multi-homing shifts the competition to a different margin: platforms compete for the same trips, so each must offer the same coverage to be usable, and the fleet that results is the sum of  fleets each sized for much of the market rather than 1/ of it. Multi-homing therefore trades a possible service benefit for a capital cost, and the capital cost is the larger of the two in a market that is already saturated. Neither regime escapes the underlying externality: a bicycle that a platform deploys occupies public space and adds to the stock that all platforms draw on, but the platform bears only its own cost of deploying it. This is the standard common-pool argument [22, 23], and in a market where the marginal bicycle adds almost no service (§5) it predicts exactly what was observed in 2017: fleets growing faster than ridership, and consolidation once the capital could no longer be financed.
8.6 What a regulator should do
The analysis suggests four measures, in descending order of value.
1. Share the data, not the bicycles. The models of Q1-Q4 require a city-wide OD matrix. Requiring platforms to deposit trip records with a neutral trustee -- as the city already does for taxi and bus data -- costs the firms nothing competitively and makes every result in this paper computable. This is the single highest-value intervention.
2. Share the fences, and pool more than concrete. Electronic fencing is public infrastructure, and the shared regime saves 7.6%-11.7% of the slot requirement relative to independent fences. But sharing fences alone does not reduce lost orders (§8), because the fleets remain separate; shared fences must be paired with shared data and a neutral rebalancing operator to matter for service.
3. Coordinate rebalancing. A neutral rebalancing operator, or a requirement that platforms' trucks serve shared depots, recovers the fixed cost that is currently paid three times.
4. Tie fleet caps to measured service. A cap expressed as bicycles per thousand trips, rather than as a fixed number, keeps the fleet anchored to the measured service requirement as demand grows and is auditable from the shared data of measure one. The scenarios of §5 do not identify an optimal fleet size, so the cap should be set conservatively and revised with the shared data.
8.7 What the external data say about the market
The OpenStreetMap check of §7 is also evidence about competition. The attractors that the model identifies -- the busiest destination cells -- are public places: subway entrances, commercial streets, university gates. They are not platform-specific. A single platform's data recovers them, as ours does, but only because that platform is large; a market of five equal platforms would give each a fifth of the evidence about the same public geography. The city is the natural owner of the model, and the argument for data sharing is an argument about who can see the city whole.

9.5 The fleet estimate
The closed fleet is a capture-recapture estimate between the nine complete days and the truncated second window: 462,893 bicycles seen in the first window, 331,822 in the second, 309,961 in both, giving 495,540 with a sampling standard error of 131 (95% interval 495,283-495,797). The closure failures dominate, and the signs are the opposite of what a first guess suggests. Heterogeneity in use is the largest: the recapture rate rises monotonically from 0.4945 for one-trip bicycles to 0.8039 for 11-or-more-trip bicycles (2 = 15,063.61 on 4 df; Cochran-Armitage  = 121.57; rate ratio 1.6257), so heavy users are over-represented in the overlap and the pooled estimate is biased down. Retirement of a recapturable bicycle biases the estimate up by 0.10533 per bicycle; new deployment between the windows biases it up by 1.49339 per bicycle; retirement of a never-used bicycle has no effect (0). A stratified Chapman estimator, which allows each use stratum its own recapture rate, returns the observed 14-day union 484,754 exactly -- by construction, not by fit -- so only   484,754 is design-independent. The Chao1 value 534,039 is not used as a lower bound: the homogeneous model reproduces all three statistics and the Chao1 value to within 0.05 bicycles, so the excess is an unequal-effort artefact, not heterogeneity, and the value survives only as a scenario. We therefore report the model interval [484,754, 506,635], whose lower end is the observed union and whose upper end is the normalised nine-day unseen-share reference. It is more than forty times wider than the sampling interval, which is the honest statement: the statistical error is negligible and the closure assumptions

26

are not. The rides-per-bicycle-per-day figure follows the same ordering: 4.33 at the 495,540 point estimate (an upper estimate, because that fleet is biased low), 4.42 at the design-independent ceiling   484,754, and 4.01 only in the 534,039 scenario. We quote 4.33 and state the ceiling alongside it.
9.6 Range of validity
The conclusions hold for a dense, flat, monocentric city on a weekday in May 2017, with at least 484,754 bicycles and 4.33-4.42 rides per bicycle per day. They should not be extrapolated to:
· systems with stations, where the docking constraint changes the inventory process and the rebalancing problem becomes a routing problem with hard capacity;
· low-density cities, where the walking radius of Q4 covers a much smaller share of arrivals and the incentive pool of Q3 vanishes;
· other layouts, where the 4,000-site, 150-slot result is specific to this 0.005 grid, this walking radius and demand surface;
· extreme weather or holidays, not represented in the fourteen recorded dates (of which five training and two held-out days drive the models; 17 May has no records);
· the long run, where the attraction  itself changes with land use. The model is a description of an equilibrium, not a forecast of one.
10 Evaluation and Conclusions
10.1 Strengths
One model, four answers. The gravity model of Q1 is the demand engine for the rebalancing program, the incentive supply curve and the parking forecast. Because the same estimated object is used four times, the answers are comparable: the same OD surface prices a truck-kilometre, a tricycle-day and a reroute, and the two-tier plan of Q2 and the incentive of Q3 are evaluated on the same demand and the same penalty. Identification is treated as part of the modelling. Four quantities that the data do not contain are handled explicitly: the sampling rate is estimated and its role as an upper-bound scale factor is stated; the fleet is estimated with capture-recapture, a stratified check and a model interval; the initial-inventory proxy is carried as two scenarios; and the incentive parameters are declared unidentified, with only 0 re-optimised and the rest as a limitation. Validation is both internal and external. The behavioural model is tested on two held-out days against two nested benchmarks and against a shared- simplification. The spatial structure is tested against an independent source -- 1,097 subway entrances from OpenStreetMap -- and the attractors the model finds are nearly twice as likely to sit near one as a random cell.
10.2 Weaknesses
Attraction is endogenous.  is measured from the arrivals it already receives, so the model describes an equilibrium rather than a structural choice model. It can answer "what if the distance decay changed?" but not "what if a new mall opened?". A structural alternative -- a random-utility model with employment and retail density as attributes -- would answer the second but requires land-use data we do not have. The incentive parameters are assumed. No reward was offered in May 2017, so , , 0 cannot be estimated from this dataset. They are fixed at  = 2,  = 0.35, 0 = 0.8, and only 0 is re-optimised. The recommended reward of 0.60 CNY is a prediction of the model, not a measurement; it is the design of a pilot, not a settled price. The Q3 truck-caliber conclusion (incentives cannot replace trucks) also flips sign in roughly 14%-16% of the grouped parameter draws of Section 9, so even its sign is conditional on the assumed parameters. The tricycle saving is not a system saving. Q3 moves trips between cells of one zone; Q2 moves bicycles between zones. Reroutes are restricted to the same zone, which is what keeps the truck layer unchanged, so the 18,011.46 CNY/day saving replaces part of the tricycle layer and nothing else. It is a deterministic singleday result on the planning midpoint; its uncertainty is expressed by the two source caps (1.08%-2.92%) and the 0 table, not by the truck-caliber intervals of Section 9. Integer programs and proxy intervals. The truck layer is solved as an integer MILP with a 1.01% LP gap, and the tricycle allocation is integer, with a rounding loss of 1,494.80 unserved departures between the LP solution and the executable schedule. The intervals of Section 9 are LP-proxy intervals: at 11 representative parameter points the integer chain deviates from the proxy by at most 38,986.30 CNY/day, about 0.2 standard
27

deviations of the Q2 total. Both are approximations, not exact statements. The plan is a bound in two directions. The 975-tricycle fleet is an upper bound on the executable minimum (the ideal LP uses 889), and the 59.385% saving is a lower bound on the ideal LP saving (61.06%). One ledger, one reward, one day. The Q3 optimum is priced on the increment ledger and checked against the 30,000 CNY budget; the payment ledger prices the same reward higher and is enforced by a conservative reservation rule. A uniform reward is modelled; a personalised one is not, and the whole answer is a single planning day, not a distribution over days. No weather, no holidays, no long run. The seven modelled days are mild spring weekdays. Rain reduces cycling sharply and would change both rates and decay; the model has nothing to say about it.
10.3 What would change the conclusions
The Q3 tricycle-caliber answer is sized by two choices. Under the conservative surplus cap the net saving falls to 6,651.22 CNY/day (1.08%); under the arrivals cap it is 18,011.46 (2.92%). Re-optimising 0 at 0.8 and 1.2 times its value moves the optimum to 0.55 and 0.70 CNY and the saving to 19,747.42 and 16,365.88. The qualitative direction survives all of these, but the magnitude does not. The Q2 fleet conclusion is narrower than it looks. The integer scan covers five grid points (0, 20, 40, 60, 80); 60 is the minimum of those points, not a proof of a global optimum. Under the joint parameter draws the P5-P50-P95 of  is 0-40-60, and about 91% of draws move the recommendation away from 60. The two-tier fleet of 975 and the 59.385% saving should therefore be read as bounds, not as a point design. The Q4 conclusion is the most robust of the five, but it is driven by the assumed cap: changing the 150-slot cap moves coverage by 46.77 percentage points, the day by 17.11 and the walking radius by 7.11. The cap binds in every joint draw (the 95th percentile of coverage is 0.824), so the qualitative reading holds; the exact percentages do not transfer to another cap. The oversupply diagnosis is the one conclusion that would be overturned wholesale if the initial-inventory proxy were very different from what we estimate. Our defence is conditional: at the initial-inventory proxy 343,638 the pre-plan cell-level unserved share is 5.84%, and at the closed 495,540 it is 3.51%; the operational shortage after the truck layer is 11.22%, and the two-tier plan reduces it to 1.875%. None of these supports a general claim that the system cannot run dry -- only that, at the modelled inventory, cost and placement are the binding problem.
10.4 Conclusions
Beijing's dockless bicycle system in May 2017 was not failing for want of bicycles. It was failing because bicycles were in the wrong places, because nobody was paying to move them, and because the space they occupied was free to the firms that put them there. The models in this paper quantify each of those statements. The behavioural model shows that demand is a superposition of five behaviours, dominated by short multipurpose riding, and that distance explains most of where riders go with a decay that varies by a quarter over the day. The two-tier rebalancing plan moves 24,949 bicycles with 60 trucks over 5,659.17 truck-kilometres, then adds 975 tricycles inside zones, for a total of 617,055.7515 CNY/day and a saving of 902,223.20 (59.385%) against the no-tricycle cell-level baseline. The incentive analysis cannot replace the trucks: at  = 2 the best mixed policy is at least 1,532 CNY/day worse than pure trucks even at zero reward. It can replace part of the tricycle layer: at  = 0.60 it removes 4,964.46 unserved departures and saves 18,011.46 CNY/day (2.92%), with a payment of 16,608.52 inside the 30,000 budget. The fence analysis places 4,000 sites of 150 slots, covering 59.53% of peak occupancy and 55.80% of arrivals at 343,638, and 52.18% and 49.59% at 495,540, with 394,553.13 bike-slots left unassigned at the measured inventory. The unifying finding is that the instruments are complements with different domains: trucks where the imbalance is between zones, tricycles and incentives where it is within a zone, fences where the parking must be bounded, and data -- shared, city-wide data -- because none of these instruments can be aimed without it. Most numbers in this paper are printed directly from result files by a single script; the constants that are typed rather than computed are listed in the Report on Use of AI Tools, and the pipeline is run in order as documented there.

Report on Use of AI Tools
Tool used
Claude Code (Anthropic), version 2.1.322, a command-line agentic coding assistant based on a large language model, used from 11 to 18 November 2026. The runtime reported the model identifier deepseek-flash[1m] for the final pass. The tool operated in a local development environment with file-system access and the ability to execute Python and typeset LATEX.
What the tool did
1. Data engineering. Wrote and debugged the geohash decoder, the cleaning pipeline that converts the 191 MB order file into a typed columnar store, and the aggregation to cells, zones and time bands.
2. Modelling and computation. Implemented the gravity model and its maximum-likelihood estimator, the user segmentation, the time-space network linear program (formulated by the authors, encoded with Gurobi's Python interface), the discrete-event simulators, the logit incentive model, the maximal-coverage siting heuristic and the Monte-Carlo capacity model.
3. Figures. Produced all six figures as vector PDFs with matplotlib. 4. Text. Drafted the LATEX manuscript, tables and reference list from the computed results under the authors'
direction and repeated review.
Representative queries and outputs
The two examples below are from the validation stage and are reproduced from the working record; the outputs are archived with the code as dest_validation.json and k_stability.json. Example 1: held-out comparison of destination models.
Query. Compare the gravity destination model with distance-only, attraction-only and uniform benchmarks on held-out days. Fit each model's own distance-decay parameter; report negative log-likelihood per trip and top-five hit rate; do not use AIC.
Output. The script fitted five time bands and scored 555,675 held-out trips. Pooled negative loglikelihood was 3.4185 for the full model, 3.4881 for distance-only, 8.1124 for attraction-only and 8.5685 for uniform; pooled top-five hit rates were 0.4255, 0.3924, 0.0108 and 0.00095. Because the attraction field is a smoothed empirical density, the comparison uses out-of-sample loss rather than AIC. The full model reduced loss by 0.0696 nats (95% bootstrap interval 0.0685-0.0705) and raised the top-five hit rate by 3.31 percentage points (3.23-3.38).
Example 2: stability of the user segmentation.
Query. Test whether the published  = 5 segmentation is stable. Run  = 3-6 with at least 20 random initialisations per ; report pairwise adjusted Rand index (mean and minimum), silhouette score, cross- agreement, and agreement with the published labels.
Output. The published  = 5 labels were recovered at ARI 0.994, but across 20 initialisations the pairwise ARI was 0.665 on average and 0.305 at worst. The best-run silhouette on a 40,000-user subsample was 0.256 at  = 5 and 0.263 at  = 6; the mean silhouette on a 10,000-user subsample was 0.251 at  = 5 and 0.246 at  = 6. The output therefore did not support the claim that the segmentation is stable across random initialisations.
What was verified, and how
· Result numbers are script-generated, with listed exceptions. Most values quoted in the text are printed by 95_numbers.py from the result files under data/. The constant 343,638 is still hard-coded in four computation scripts and two figure lines: 20_q2_diag.py line 11, used at line 26 for the initial inventory written to q2_state.npz; 24_cell_curve.py line 9, used at line 36 for the cell-level initial inventory; 21_q2_opt.py line 19, where it cancels algebraically; 30_q3_incentive.py line 16, where it is declared but unused; and 90_figs.py lines 256 and 258, the cost curve. It is an input to the Q2/Q5 runs, not a display value. The Q5 interpretation text in 50_q5_platform.py lines 37-38 is also written by hand. The paper keeps 343,638 only as the measured initial inventory, and does not use it as a fleet estimate. The two full-coverage slot bands 600,000-750,000 and 780,000-900,000 in t009_q4.py are typed planning bands, used only as sanity checks.
· The computation is reproducible in a fixed order, not by a single command. After the preparation scripts 00-04, the numbered stages are run from the project root in the order 10  11  20  21  ...
